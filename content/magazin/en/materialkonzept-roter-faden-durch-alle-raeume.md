# A Material Concept for Your Home: One Common Thread Through Every Room

**In short:** A material concept decides, for the whole apartment, which few materials carry the rooms, which accompany them, and where an accent is placed on purpose. Instead of deciding afresh in every room, you get one common thread running through flooring, timber, stone, metal and textiles. The reward is a home where the eye finally rests, surfaces age with dignity and every room belongs to the next.

You step into your hallway and, without quite knowing why, your shoulders rise. Everything here is lovely, and nothing is at rest. Now picture the same hallway with one floor, one timber and one metal tone, and the eye settling the moment you walk in.

Imagine a couple in Mariahilf who have lived in their apartment for eight years, every piece chosen with care. Standing in the hallway, we count the materials in view together: oak-effect laminate, stone-effect tiles, a kitchen in white high gloss, a worktop in black granite, a walnut dining table, chairs with chrome frames, a pine shelf, stainless steel handles, brass lights, a sofa in grey upholstery, a rug with an oriental pattern. Eleven materials in a single glance. "We've always wondered," she says, "why it never feels quite calm here."

## What a material concept for your home is

A material concept is a deliberately limited selection of materials, surfaces and colours that applies to all the rooms of an apartment. It determines which material forms the floor, which timber recurs in built-ins and furniture, which stone or ceramic is used in kitchen and bathroom, which metal tone links handles, taps and lights, and which textiles soften the whole.

The idea behind it is simple. A room feels calm when the eye recognises repetition and does not have to process new stimuli all the time. An apartment feels connected when that repetition carries on from room to room. The common thread comes not from uniformity but from an order you sense without having to name it.

The material concept is one building block of the [whole-home design concept](/magazin/gesamtkonzept-wohnung-was-bedeutet-das/). It is closely linked to the colour concept, described in the article [A colour concept for the whole apartment](/magazin/farbkonzept-wohnung-ruhe-statt-trend/). And it is a subject on which, with Viennese joinery tradition since 1935 behind us, we speak from our own workbench. But why does a room full of good things feel so restless?

## Why too many materials make a room restless

Hardly anyone deliberately plans eleven materials in one room. They accumulate because each decision was made individually. The kitchen was chosen at the kitchen studio, the floor from the flooring fitter, the table from the furniture retailer, the light in a shop in the first district. Each of these pieces was a good choice in itself.

The problem is the sum. Every material has its own colour, its own degree of gloss, its own texture and its own temperature. Oak is warm and lively, high gloss is cool and smooth, granite is dark and heavy, chrome is hard and reflective. When all of that is in view at once, a kind of noise arises that you experience as restlessness, even if you cannot say exactly where it comes from.

Then there are imitations. Wood-effect laminate next to real walnut, stone-effect tiles next to real granite: the eye sees the difference, and the proximity makes both look worse.

> Calm in a room is rarely a matter of few things. It is a matter of few materials.

**The quiet price of having no plan.** You pay a small tax every day: an eye that never rests, a corner you avoid, a new purchase that makes the pile one material bigger. Without an order, each good decision adds to the noise, and the longer the pieces accumulate, the harder it is to pull them together. So what does an order look like?

## Base, companion, accent: the order of a material concept

In practice we work with a simple order that proves itself in almost every apartment. There are base materials that carry large surfaces, companions that recur and connect, and a few accents that stand out on purpose.

| Role | Task | Typical examples | Share of the room |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base | carries the room, forms the background | floor, walls, large cupboard fronts | large |
| Companion | connects rooms, recurs | one timber for built-ins, one stone or ceramic, one metal tone | medium |
| Accent | sets a deliberate contrast | a special stone at the vanity unit, a coloured wall, a striking light | small |
| Textile | softens, improves acoustics | curtains, rugs, upholstery | supplementary |

As a rule of thumb, most apartments manage well with two to three base materials, two to three companions and one to two accents per room. That is not a rigid rule, but it is a good guide when you sense that it is becoming too much. Four of these materials do most of the work, and they appear in almost every room.

## The four threads: floor, timber, stone and metal

A material concept is carried most strongly by four elements that appear in almost every room.

### The floor
The floor is the largest continuous surface in an apartment and therefore the most important base material. A continuous finish in all living areas joins the rooms and makes the apartment feel larger. Whether parquet, terrazzo, ceramic or natural stone suits best depends on use and the existing building. The possibilities are described in the articles on [continuous floor coverings](/magazin/bodenbelag-durchgaengig-raeume-verbinden/) and on [terrazzo as a Viennese classic](/magazin/terrazzo-comeback-boden-waschtisch/). Picture walking barefoot from bedroom to kitchen on one oiled oak floor, with no threshold to stop you and the same warmth underfoot in every room.

### The timber
A timber that recurs in built-ins, furniture and perhaps doors is one of the strongest companions. Oak is light, calm and versatile, walnut is darker and warmer, ash has a lively grain. What matters is to decide on one species and use it consistently. The differences are described in the article on [solid wood in interior fit-out](/magazin/echtholz-im-innenausbau-eiche-nuss/). Whether veneer or solid timber is the right choice is explained in the article [Veneer or solid timber](/magazin/furnier-vs-massivholz-ehrlich/).

### The stone
Stone or ceramic mostly appears in kitchen and bathroom. If worktop and vanity unit are made of the same material or the same material family, a link arises between two rooms that you never see at the same time but experience one after the other. Dekton by Cosentino suits this well, because it is equally robust in kitchen and bathroom. Its properties are described in the article [Dekton explained](/magazin/dekton-was-ist-das/). For the bathroom, it is worth looking at [large-format ceramic](/magazin/grossformatige-fliesen-bad-fugen/).

### The metal
The metal tone is the smallest of the four threads and the one most often overlooked. Handles, taps, lights, fittings, chair legs, picture frames: if chrome, stainless steel, brass and black are all mixed up here, the room feels restless. A single metal tone, for example brushed stainless steel or brushed brass, joins kitchen, bathroom and living areas. More in the article on [taps and their finishes](/magazin/armaturen-auswahl-messing-edelstahl/). And once the surfaces are chosen, one question remains: how will they look in ten years?

## Material and daily life: how surfaces age

A material concept must look good not only on the day of handover but after ten years. That is why we ask not only how a material looks but also how it ages and how it is treated in daily life.

Some materials become more beautiful over time. Oiled timber develops a patina, brass darkens, natural stone grows softer in expression. Other materials look best on the first day and then lose out. High-gloss surfaces show every fingerprint, delicate lacquers pick up dents, and imitations give themselves away as soon as the surface is damaged.

For a family with small children the trade-off looks different from that for a couple without children. Anyone who cooks a lot needs a worktop that can take hot pans and knives. Honest assessments of durability are given in the article [Easy to care for? Which surfaces still look good after five years](/magazin/oberflaechen-pflegeleicht-ehrlich-bewertet/). Why longevity is the most sustainable quality of a material is described in the article [Sustainable means long-lasting](/magazin/nachhaltige-materialien-langlebigkeit/). One more thing changes every material, though: the light it sits in.

## Material and light

No material looks the same under every light. An oak that looks warm and golden in the showroom under halogen light can look grey in a north-facing room in a Viennese November. A white matt lacquer looks cool in the morning and creamy in the evening under warm light.

That is why we always assess materials in the room where they will be used, and at different times of day. Samples should be large enough to give an impression, and laid side by side so you can see how they work together. The colour temperature of the future lighting also plays a part. A material concept and a lighting concept should therefore be developed together, as described in the article on the [basics of lighting design](/magazin/lichtplanung-wohnung-grundlagen/). So how do you get from all these ideas to decisions?

## Developing a material concept: step by step

A material concept does not emerge from a catalogue, but in a fixed order:

1. **Check what exists:** What stays? A lovely old parquet, a tiled stove or a beloved piece of furniture often set the direction.
2. **Clarify daily life:** Who lives here, how is cooking done, are there children or pets? That determines how robust the materials need to be.
3. **Set the base:** Floor and wall colour as the largest surfaces first.
4. **Choose the timber:** One species for built-ins and furniture, matching the floor.
5. **Decide on stone or ceramic:** For kitchen and bathroom, ideally from one family.
6. **Decide the metal tone:** One tone for handles, taps, lights and fittings.
7. **Place accents:** A few deliberately chosen contrasts.
8. **Add textiles:** Curtains, rugs and upholstery that soften the concept.
9. **Check samples on site:** In daylight and artificial light, side by side, in sufficient size.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a material concept?
A material concept is a deliberately limited selection of materials, surfaces and colours that applies to all the rooms of an apartment. It fixes floor, timber, stone, metal tone and textiles and arranges them as base, companion and accent. This creates a common thread that joins the rooms.

### How many materials should you use in a room?
As a guide, two to three base materials, two to three companions and one to two accents per room are usually enough. That is not a rigid rule. What matters is that materials repeat from room to room and that not every room starts afresh.

### Does the floor have to be the same in every room?
Not necessarily, but a continuous floor in the living areas is one of the most effective means to a calm apartment. In bathroom and kitchen a different material can make sense. It should then match the main floor in colour and effect. A walk through your rooms shows quickly which solution fits.

### Can you combine different species of timber?
Yes, but with care. One species should lead and recur in built-ins and floor. A second timber can serve as a deliberate accent, for example a walnut table in an oak apartment. Many different timbers side by side quickly feel restless. Tell us which timber you love, and we will show you how it can stay.

### Should all metal surfaces be the same?
A single metal tone for handles, taps and lights noticeably joins the rooms. Small departures, such as a light in a different tone, can work as an accent. But when chrome, brass, stainless steel and black all appear at once, the room usually feels restless.

### How do you check whether materials go together?
With samples large enough to be judged side by side in your own room, in daylight and in the evening under artificial light. Small samples in a showroom often give a false impression. With timber it is also worth considering how it will age. Not sure? Bring photos, and we look at them together in the first call.

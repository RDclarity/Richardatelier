Whole-Home Design Concept: One Plan, One Home That Feels Finished

By Richard DobrohruschkaUpdated 2026-11-05

In short: A whole-home design concept plans layout, lighting, materials, built-ins, furnishings and the order of the trades as one project, instead of deciding room by room. It asks for more planning at the start, and it usually repays that through fewer duplicated jobs, corrections and wrong purchases. What you gain is a home that feels as though it was made in one piece, with one person answering for all of it.

The post lands on the hall floor again because there is nowhere else for it to go. Your reading lamp trails its cable across the parquet like a trip wire. Picture a couple who have just bought a 110-square-metre apartment in Döbling, built in 1928, with herringbone parquet under wall-to-wall carpet, a kitchen from the nineties and a bathroom with a tub and pink tiles. This is the moment that decides whether, in three years' time, the apartment is a home or a collection of individual solutions.

What a whole-home design concept is

A whole-home concept is a plan that relates every room and every decision in an apartment to one another. It does not begin by asking which kitchen you like, but by asking how the people in this apartment want to live. The layout, zones, lighting, materials and furniture follow from the answer.

The difference from conventional furnishing lies in the order of work. Those who furnish fill rooms. Those who develop a whole-home concept first establish the rhythms of daily life and build the rooms around them. The kitchen is then part of the movement between hallway, dining area and living room, not just a product.

In practice, a whole-home concept also includes responsibility for delivery. At Richard Atelier that means one point of contact from the first sketch to handover, supported by a Viennese joinery tradition since 1935 and by our own workshop, Tischlerkultur. Piecemeal work: how apartments come together in stages

Back to our couple in Döbling. Say they order the kitchen first from a kitchen studio. The kitchen turns out beautiful, but in its old position, because nobody asked whether the wall to the living room should stay. A year later the bathroom follows. The plumber finds that the riser pipe has to be replaced anyway, and that the freshly tiled kitchen wall would need to be opened up to do it.

Then come the wardrobes. The joiner plans them soundly, but the timber does not match the parquet, and the handles do not match the kitchen. The living room has no socket where the sofa now stands, so the reading lamp hangs on an extension cable.

Piecemeal work is rarely a question of taste. It is a question of sequence.

None of these decisions was wrong in itself. The trouble is that each one narrows the next, and nobody holds the whole in view. In the end the couple have spent more than planned and still do not feel quite arrived in their own home.

Doing nothing has a cost too, and it arrives in small instalments. You work around the missing socket. You carry the same chair from room to room. You get used to a kitchen that almost works. Each compromise is tiny; together they decide how the apartment feels every morning. And a wrong choice is rarely a drama, only a long one: walls opened twice, a wardrobe rebuilt, a floor protected for the third time. What would have changed, had someone asked one question at the very start?

The guiding question: how do you want to live?

Every one of our projects begins with the same question: how do you want to live? Not: how should the room look? The answer is rarely a style, but rather a series of situations. Who cooks, and when? Who comes out of the bathroom at 6:40 a.m. while the other is already making coffee? Where do keys, post and school bags end up when everyone gets home?

These situations become requirements. If the couple like to cook for friends, the kitchen needs a surface where guests can stand without being in the way. If one of them works from home two days a week, the apartment needs a quiet spot that disappears in the evening. If both get up early, the position of the bathroom decides the mood of the morning.

Now imagine it finished. It is 6:40 on a Tuesday. The bathroom door closes without a sound, the coffee machine is warm in its niche beside the fridge, and morning light runs along the parquet all the way to the kitchen. Keys and school bags have their places by the door. You do not think about any of it; you simply feel that the apartment is on your side.

Only once these situations are clear does the question of material and colour make sense. A whole-home concept therefore translates life situations into rooms. That sounds abstract, but it is very concrete: it decides whether the coffee machine stands next to the fridge or next to the dining table. And what goes into such a concept, exactly?

What a whole-home concept covers

The scope depends on the apartment and on how far the intervention goes. Typically, these building blocks are part of it:

  • Survey and layout analysis: measured survey, structural questions, pipes and cables, windows, ceiling heights, the condition of floors and walls.
  • Zoning and transitions between rooms: which rooms stay, which open up, where a sliding door is needed instead of a wall.
  • Lighting concept: ambient light, task light, accents and controls, tuned to daylight and use. More on this in the basics of lighting design.
  • Material and colour concept: floors, timber, stone, ceramics, metals and wall colours as one coherent palette.
  • Bespoke built-ins: kitchen, wardrobes, dressing room, washbasin unit, hallway storage, bookcase wall.
  • Furnishings and lighting fixtures: sofa, table, chairs, beds, rugs and curtains.
  • Building services coordination: electrical planning, plumbing, heating, ventilation, possibly smart home.
  • Coordination of trades, budget and schedule: who does what and when, what it costs, and who stands behind it.

Not every project needs every building block in full depth. An apartment whose floors and pipework are in good condition needs mainly built-ins, lighting and furnishings. A complete renovation of an Altbau, the pre-1945 Viennese apartment building, needs everything. Why a whole-home concept costs less than piecemeal work

More planning sounds like more cost. In practice it often works the other way round, because the expensive parts of a renovation are the ones that never appear as a line on an invoice.

Cost factorPiecemealWhole-home concept
Site set-uprepeated, anew for every stageonce, for all trades
Pipes, cables and connectionsoften added later, walls opened twiceplanned once, installed once
Wrong purchasesfurniture and lighting that do not fitselected in context
Correctionssockets, lighting, interfaces between tradessolved in the plan
Purchasingindividual orders per projectcoordinated deliveries and installation dates
Time and nervesevery stage is a new building siteone building phase with a clear end

The biggest item is usually the building site itself. Every time floors have to be protected, dust screens put up and tradespeople coordinated, costs arise that have nothing to do with the actual result. If you have three stages, you pay for that three times.

Then there is the electrics. Many annoyances in finished apartments come down to sockets, switches and light outlets in the wrong place. If the furnishing is known before the electrician chases the walls, they disappear. Why this matters so much is explained in the article on electrical planning, where the socket is planned in front of the sofa.

Finally, purchasing. If you plan kitchen, wardrobes, lighting and upholstered furniture together, deliveries can be combined and installations scheduled to follow one another. This is not a discount promotion; it is simply more efficient. Which leaves the question of how the work actually runs.

The course of a whole-home concept in seven steps

The order is always the same; the duration of each phase depends on the size of the apartment and the scope of the work.

  1. Initial conversation: A free 30-minute call by phone or video. We listen, ask questions and clarify whether we are a good match.
  2. Project sheet: You send us photos, a floor plan and your wish list. From these we draw up a first assessment of scope and budget range.
  3. On-site survey: Measuring, inspection of pipes, cables and building fabric, with a structural engineer or master builder where needed.
  4. Concept: Layout, zones, lighting idea and material palette. This is where the question of how you want to live is answered.
  5. Detailed design: Working drawings for built-ins, electrics and plumbing, selection of furniture and lighting, cost schedule.
  6. Implementation: Coordination of all trades. The built-ins are made by our joinery workshop Tischlerkultur, which has been family-run since 1935.
  7. Handover: Joint acceptance, a walk-through of how everything works, and snagging. After that, you live there.

For our couple in Döbling this means: the kitchen is not ordered first but thought through first. Perhaps it moves into the room that is currently the dining room, and the wall between becomes a wide opening. The bathroom gets the new pipework at the same time, which was due anyway. Which timeframes are realistic for the individual phases is shown in the realistic schedule for an apartment renovation.

Now picture the day after handover. Afternoon light runs through the wide opening between kitchen and dining room. A drawer glides shut under your fingertips, its front matching the timber of the wardrobe two doors down. Friends arrive at seven, someone remarks that the apartment feels calm, and nobody can say why. You can: it was planned as one. So what does that cost?

Costs, budget and responsibility

A whole-home concept has no fixed price, because no two apartments are alike. The cost depends on floor area, depth of intervention and the share of bespoke work, and a reliable estimate comes in the first consultation.

What counts is the scope: the size of the kitchen and bathroom, the appliances and fittings, the depth of intervention. Built-ins, floors, lighting and furnishings come on top. An honest breakdown is in the article What does a whole-home concept cost?.

More important than any single figure is control. The budget is allocated at the outset: where is investment worthwhile, and where is a solid solution enough? Perhaps more goes into the kitchen, where life happens, and less into the guest room, occupied ten nights a year. That trade-off is only possible when all items are on one sheet.

A budget is not a ceiling. It is a decision about what matters to you.

Responsibility rests with one partner. When planning and execution belong together, there are no interfaces at which planner, joiner and plumber pass the blame to one another. What this means in the day-to-day of a building site is described in the article on planning and execution from a single source. Examples of completed projects can be found in our references. When a whole-home concept is especially worthwhile

Not every apartment needs a whole-home concept. If you only want to renew a bathroom and are happy with the rest, a targeted bathroom renovation will serve you well. There are, however, situations in which a whole-home concept is almost always the better choice.

The first is the purchase of an existing apartment. While it is empty, pipes, floors and layout can be renewed in one go. Later, with furniture and daily life in place, every building project costs more and wears you down.

The second is a change of life stage: a child arrives, the children move out, someone will work from home. The old layout often no longer fits, and single new pieces of furniture do not fix that.

The third is the wish for calm. Many people only realise after years that their apartment feels restless even though every single piece is beautiful. Usually this comes down to too many materials and too little storage. A well-thought-out storage concept for the whole apartment and a clear material palette change that fundamentally. Bespoke fitted wardrobes and a fitted kitchen that suit the rest of the apartment are the most important tools here.

Frequently asked questions

What is a whole-home design concept?

A whole-home concept plans all the rooms of an apartment as one connected whole: layout, zones, lighting, materials, built-ins, furnishings and the coordination of the tradespeople. The starting point is the question of how the residents want to live. The result is a home in which all the parts are tuned to one another, with one contact from first sketch to handover.

Is a whole-home concept only for large apartments?

No. In smaller apartments in particular, every square metre is valuable and wrong decisions weigh more heavily. There, a whole-home concept helps above all with storage, light and proportions, and the scope adapts to the size of the apartment. Not sure yours qualifies? The free first call will tell you.

How much does a whole-home concept cost for an apartment in Vienna?

There is no fixed price, because floor area and depth of intervention vary widely. The construction cost itself depends on whether the work is furnishing only or renovation as well, and it is itemised transparently as the project progresses. An honest first estimate comes in the free 30-minute initial call, without obligation.

Can I carry out a whole-home concept in stages?

Yes, that is possible and sometimes sensible. What matters is that the plan for the whole is in place first and the stages build on it. For example, the pipework for a later kitchen can be laid at the same time as the bathroom renovation. That way, nothing you do now has to be undone later.

How long does a whole-home concept take from idea to moving in?

That depends on the scope. For furnishing alone with built-ins it is often a few months; for a complete renovation with a planning phase, more like nine months or longer. Lead times for kitchens and Italian furniture play an important part. We set out the schedule with you at the start, so you know what to expect.

What is the difference between an interior architect and furnishing advice?

Furnishing advice usually selects furniture and colours for existing rooms. A whole-home concept goes deeper: it can change the layout, plans lighting and electrics, designs built-ins and takes responsibility for delivery. At Richard Atelier the built-ins come from our own joinery workshop, and one person stays your contact throughout.

Colour Scheme for Your Home: Calm Over Trend, a Palette That Lasts

By Richard DobrohruschkaUpdated 2026-11-05

Book a free consultationOr call directly: +43 660 3607188
In short: A colour scheme for your home is a coordinated palette of a few base tones that treats walls, ceilings, timber, stone and textiles as one whole. It grows out of the light in your rooms, the fixed materials and the way you live, not out of the colour of the year. The result is a home that still feels calm after ten years, where every open door leads into the same quiet palette.

You stand at the end of the hallway with every door open, and your eye jumps from sage to dove blue to ochre and never settles. Each colour is lovely on its own. Now picture the same view in one calm palette, so that you look through three rooms and feel nothing but space. Picture a couple in an Altbau apartment (the pre-1945 Viennese apartment building) in Wieden: 110 square metres, three rooms facing the street, two facing the courtyard. When they moved in, they painted every room on its own: the living room in a sage green from a magazine, the bedroom in dove blue, the hallway white, the kitchen in a warm ochre. This is exactly where a colour scheme for the home begins.

What a colour scheme for the home is, and what it is not

A colour scheme is not a list of wall colours. It is a decision about what role colour should play in a home in the first place. Some people want colour to step back and let timber, stone and light speak. Others want a single room that is bold and individual while the rest stays quiet.

So we do not start with the colour fan deck, but with the question behind every one of our projects: how do you want to live? Someone who wakes at six in a north-facing bedroom needs different tones from someone who mostly reads there in the evening. Someone with many books, pictures and keepsakes needs walls that carry them rather than compete with them.

A good colour scheme has three qualities:

  • It is limited. Three to five base tones for the whole apartment, plus gradations within the same family.
  • It includes the fixed materials. Parquet, worktop, tiles and cabinet fronts all have colour, even if we do not call it that.
  • It thinks in sightlines. What matters is not the individual room, but what you see when you look from one room into the next.

This makes the colour scheme a close relative of the material concept as a common thread. In practice we develop the two together, because an oak tone is never quite finished without the matching wall colour. So why do trend colours age so quickly?

Why trend colours date so quickly at home

Trend colours are born at trade fairs, in campaigns and on social media. They are made to look good in a photograph, in a well-lit studio, with a single sofa in front of them. A home is the opposite: it is lived in at every time of day, in every kind of weather and every kind of mood.

There is also a simple effect at work: what you see everywhere becomes tiring. The sage green that looked fresh three years ago now reads as the colour of one particular season. That is not the fault of green; it was chosen without any relationship to the room.

Living with a restless palette has a price you pay daily without noticing. The eye never rests in the corridor, the bedroom you wanted as a retreat does not feel like one, and every repaint costs another weekend of tape and dust sheets. A wrong tone in a fixed material is harder still, because nobody repaints a floor or a stone worktop next spring.

A colour does not date because it goes out of fashion. It dates because it never belonged to the room.

That does not mean a calm colour scheme has to be dull. It only means that colour should come from the place itself: from the light, from the materials, from the architecture of the building.

Imagine it done. You step into the hall on a grey November afternoon, and the walls hold the light like warm paper. The oak underfoot is a shade deeper than the wall, the doors share one quiet tone, and your eye runs from the front door through three rooms without a break. Nothing shouts. You exhale. It starts with the light.

Light decides first

The same colour looks completely different in two rooms of the same apartment. In a Viennese Altbau with tall windows facing a south-facing street, a warm grey looks almost beige. In the room facing the shaded light well, it tips towards green and feels cool.

Our couple in Wieden show this clearly. The courtyard bedroom faces north and gets only a little indirect light early in the morning, so the dove blue looks grey and heavy. In the street-facing living room, the sage green takes on a yellowish cast in the afternoon that makes the room restless.

For the colour scheme, this means:

  • North-facing rooms cope better with warm tones with a slight red or yellow component than with cool greys.
  • South-facing rooms can afford to be cooler, because the light warms them anyway.
  • Rooms with little daylight benefit from a higher light reflectance value, that is, a colour that throws more light back.

The light reflectance value tells you how much light a surface reflects, on a scale from 0 (black) to 100 (pure white). For rooms with little daylight we usually recommend wall tones in the upper range, without going straight to sterile white. You can read more about this interplay in our article on daylight, windows, mirrors and colour.

Artificial light changes every colour too. Bulbs at 2,700 kelvin warm, 4,000 kelvin cools. If you fix the colour scheme without considering the lighting plan for your home, you are in effect choosing two different colours: one for the day and one for the evening. But light is only half the palette. What about everything that stays in the room for decades?

Fixed materials are part of the palette

Most people think of walls when they think of colour. Yet walls are only part of what you perceive. An oiled oak parquet, a worktop in Cosentino Dekton, a wall of fitted wardrobes, a sofa, a large rug: all of these have colour, and most of them stay far longer than any coat of paint.

We therefore work in a fixed order, from the permanent to the changeable:

  1. Floor. It is the largest continuous surface and often stays for decades.
  2. Fixed built-ins. Kitchen, fitted wardrobes, bathroom, doors. Here it is about the choice of timber, lacquer colour and stone tone.
  3. Large furniture and textiles. Sofa, curtains, rugs.
  4. Walls and ceilings. They tie together everything that came before.
  5. Accents. Art, cushions, individual objects. These may change.

Whoever works the other way round and picks the wall colour first ends up searching desperately for a timber that goes with it. With bespoke fitted wardrobes and kitchens, the tone of the fronts can be matched exactly to the wall, which is often possible only to a limited extent with off-the-shelf systems. That is the advantage of one hand: our own workshop, Tischlerkultur, family-run in Vienna since 1935, builds the fronts, and the person who chose the tone answers for the result.

A note on timber: oak becomes slightly warmer and darker over time, walnut tends to become lighter. This should feed into the palette. In our article on real wood in interior fit-out we explain how different woods age. Once the permanent things are set, how do the wall colours follow?

Coordinating wall colours: base tone, companion, accent

For practical work, a simple structure has proved its worth. It is not a rigid recipe, but a good order.

RoleShare of surfacesTypical choiceWhere it appears
Base toneroughly 60 to 70%broken white, warm light grey, lime toneHallways, living spaces, ceilings
Companionroughly 20 to 30%one step darker or richer from the same familyBedroom, niches, back walls
Accentno more than 10%a distinct, muted toneOne room, one wall, one piece of furniture

The base tone carries the apartment. It should be so similar in all rooms that you feel no break when you look through an open door. It can be exactly the same tone or a slight variation, depending on the light.

The companion gives rooms with their own function a mood of their own. In the bedroom, for example, a deeper, warmer tone that makes the room feel more enclosed and sheltered in the evening. We describe in detail how such a retreat is built up in our article on the bedroom as a place of rest.

The accent is deliberately sparing. It belongs in a place where you linger: in the reading corner, the guest WC, a library. There it may be strong, because it does not compete with the rest of the apartment.

Do not forget the ceiling

In Viennese Altbau apartments with ceilings of 3.20 metres and more, the ceiling is a large surface in its own right. Pure white often looks harsh there, especially next to warm walls. A hint of the wall tone in the ceiling colour, about a quarter of the pigmentation, makes the room feel more enclosed and calmer. In low rooms, on the other hand, a lighter ceiling helps so that it does not press down. And where one colour ends and the next begins matters even more.

Transitions between rooms

A colour scheme is decided at the thresholds. Where two rooms meet determines whether an apartment reads as connected rooms or as a collection of separate ones.

A few rules that have proved their worth:

  • Change colour at edges, not across a surface. A new colour begins at a door reveal or a room corner, never in the middle of a continuous wall.
  • Doors and frames in one tone. In an apartment with many doors, it is enormously calming when all of them share the same colour.
  • Open areas as one room. Where kitchen, dining and living flow into one another, the base tone runs throughout.

How to plan such thresholds deliberately is shown in our basics article on room transitions. Colour is one of several tools there, alongside flooring, light and built-ins. But how do you choose between two nearly identical whites?

Colour samples: how to decide properly

No colour fan deck and no screen shows how a colour will look in your room, so we always work with large sample patches directly on the wall.

  1. Paint them large enough. At least 50 by 50 centimetres, better a square metre. Small patches deceive.
  2. Test on several walls. Once on the window wall, once opposite. The differences are often surprising.
  3. Look at them at three times of day. Morning, midday, evening under artificial light.
  4. Compare with the fixed materials. Hold a parquet sample, a front sample, a fabric swatch directly in front of it.
  5. Live with them for a few days. The first impression is rarely the right one. After a week with two samples, you usually know which one should stay.

For our couple in Wieden, we placed three warm, slightly broken whites side by side for the base tone. On the first evening, both preferred the lightest. After four days it was the middle one, because it did not turn grey in the courtyard room.

The right colour is the one you stop noticing after a week and would still miss if it were gone.

Colour in an Altbau: surface and substrate

In a Viennese Altbau, it is not only the tone that matters but also the paint itself. Old lime plasters have different properties from modern gypsum plasters. Whether lime paint, silicate paint or emulsion is the right choice depends on the substrate and the room. We settle that together with the painter on site before any tone is fixed.

The sheen changes the effect too. Matt surfaces look calm and deep, forgive small irregularities in old plaster and reflect no light. Satin-matt surfaces are somewhat more hard-wearing but show every dent. In hallways and children's rooms, a washable matt product is worthwhile.

If you are renovating an Altbau apartment anyway, you should plan the colour scheme with the renovation and not afterwards. In our article on the renovation of an Altbau apartment in Vienna we show why stucco, box-type windows and double-leaf doors call for a colour logic of their own. And once the base is calm, where does personality come in?

Textiles and art as the movable layer

The loveliest quality of a calm base scheme is that it leaves room for change. Curtains, cushions, rugs and art are the movable layer; they may be bolder and more personal, because swapping them needs no painting.

There is a practical effect as well: textiles improve the acoustics and make rooms feel more homely. A heavy linen curtain in a tone from the palette combines colour and acoustics in a single element. You will find more in our article on textiles, curtains and acoustics.

It is Sunday evening. The heavy linen curtain is drawn, a lamp at 2,700 kelvin warms the base tone, and the room swallows the noise of the street. You sit with a book, the wool rug under your feet, and nothing in the room asks anything of you.

Our couple's sage green, incidentally, has not disappeared. It now lives in a large wool rug in the living room and in an armchair by the window. There it is an accent and no longer a wall. The apartment feels calmer as a result, and the green looks lovelier than before. Frequently asked questions

How many colours should a colour scheme for the home have?

Three to five base tones are usually enough for the whole apartment, plus gradations from the same colour family. More important than the number is that timber, stone and textiles are included in this palette. Too many independent wall colours make a home fall apart, while a short palette lets every room belong to the others.

Does every room need the same wall colour?

No, but the base tone should be related in all rooms, so that no break appears when you look through open doors. Rooms with their own function, such as the bedroom, may have a deeper companion tone. Depending on the direction of the light, we often vary the same tone slightly so that it looks the same everywhere. The result is a flow you feel rather than see.

Which wall colour suits a north-facing room?

North-facing rooms receive cool, indirect light. Warm tones with a slight yellow or red component balance this out, whereas cool greys often look dull and heavy there. A higher light reflectance value also helps to keep the available light in the room. With the right tone, even a north-facing room feels warm and welcoming.

When in the project is the colour scheme fixed?

Ideally in parallel with the material concept, once the direction of the floor, kitchen and built-ins is settled. We decide the final wall colour with sample patches on site, as soon as the rooms are plastered. That way you see the tone in the real light and next to the real materials, and you decide with confidence.

Isn't white the safest choice?

White is not just white. Pure white often looks cold and harsh next to timber and warm light, whereas broken whites with a hint of grey, ochre or red are considerably more homely. A well-chosen white can be an excellent base tone, but it needs the same care as any other colour. In the first conversation we can look together at which white suits your light.

How do I include a favourite colour without it dominating the home?

Give it a specific place: an accent room such as the library or the guest WC, a piece of furniture or a rug. There it may be strong, because it does not compete with the rest. That way it stays special instead of wearing you out in everyday life, and you enjoy it every time you see it.
